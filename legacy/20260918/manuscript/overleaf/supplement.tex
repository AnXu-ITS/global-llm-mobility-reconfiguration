\section{Statistical and outcome conventions}

\subsection{Fixed-panel estimand and random streams}

The runner initializes a fresh \nolinkurl{random.Random(seed)} for every scenario and visits aircraft in sorted order. Each aircraft consumes an endurance draw, and each busy aircraft consumes an additional progress draw. SUMO also reuses the same seed. Workload-dependent draw counts change the realizations, but they do not create independently keyed scenario streams. We therefore retain dependence within each seed across the fixed scenario panel and pair policies by scenario and seed. This decision follows the implementation, rather than seed labels or state hashes alone.

For a complete panel, each seed contributes the average of its scenario-specific outcomes or paired differences. Means keep equal scenario--run weights. A t interval uses the standard deviation across 20 seed averages (10 in the E1 ablation). For a conditional endpoint with $N$ retained pairs, let $\bar d$ denote the original pair-weighted mean and $K$ the number of represented seeds. The cluster contribution is
\[
B_s=\bar d+\frac{K}{N}\sum_{i:\,s_i=s}(d_i-\bar d).
\]
The interval is $\bar d\pm t_{.975,K-1}\operatorname{sd}(B_s)/\sqrt K$. This retains the mean over jointly affected pairs without reweighting seeds that have different exposure counts. Conditional populations can differ by policy. At Site C, B4b has 201 affected runs, whereas the B4b--B2 recovery comparison includes 200 jointly affected pairs.

Continuous tests use these seed contributions, with the original signed-rank fallback for constant differences. Binary tests enumerate policy-label swaps of whole seed blocks and use the total discordant paired difference as the test statistic. This preserves pairing without assuming independent pairs for McNemar inference. Scenarios, endpoints and correction families are not selected by their p-values. Intervals are unadjusted. Holm-adjusted p-values retain the original three E1 ablation, 12 E2 and 12 E3 compound endpoints per site. E1 scenario-specific and E4 single-scenario comparisons keep their original seed units.

The E2 family includes completion, recovery time, replan latency, incumbent damage, candidate reduction, deadline violation, restoration, recovered completion, ground fallback, failure-induced fallback, necessary-fallback correctness and air intervention. E3 compares B4b with B1/B2 at L2--L4 for SWL and CRITICAL+HIGH loss.

\subsection{Service-loss audit}

All 2,880 E3 run registries were checked against their recorded horizons: 900 s for L1/L2 and 1,200 s for L3/L4. At each site, 1,440 task instances had deadlines at or before the corresponding horizon, and all were COMPLETED. Across sites, zero overdue tasks remained EN\_ROUTE or ASSIGNED. The audit therefore requires no correction to SWL for overdue ongoing tasks. Background missions with 1,800 s deadlines fall outside both horizons; remaining in service does not by itself constitute a failure. Incumbent damage remains a count of damaged final states.

As a complementary E1 measure, we linked recorded preemption times to the incumbent identified in the candidate log and its final interrupted state. At A, exposure from preemption to the horizon for persistently interrupted services averages 35 s per run for B2 and 80 s for B4b (B1: 300 s). This measure is truncated at the horizon; it does not capture the complete cumulative cost of interruption. Mission CSV records omit some incumbent preemption transitions, so a logged interruption duration of zero would be misleading. Initial incumbent deadlines are 1,800 s, beyond the 900 s horizon. This exposure measure therefore supports no claim about incumbent lateness.

\section{Technical checks and timing sensitivity}

\subsection{Duplicate pending commands and E4B regression}

The original B0 D60 pipeline records a GROUND\_FALLBACK command at 300 s, due at 360 s. At 360 s, the fault triggers a new decision with the same normalized action and the same 181.763 s ETA field. The original queue replaces the pending command and restarts the 60 s wait. It issues the command at 420 s, and transport completes at 602 s. This extra wait produced the original 302 s completion duration.

The revised queue keeps the original due time when a validated normalized action exactly matches a pending command. A changed action still supersedes the pending command, and the surviving command is revalidated at execution. The \nolinkurl{queue_fix.py} overlay implements this correction without changing the original project source or raw runs.

All 20 B0 D60 seeds were rerun through SUMO--BlueSky. The command issued at 360 s, and transport completed at 542 s, giving a duration of 242 s. Single-seed D00/D30 controls retain 182/212 s; fault times 359/361 s both give 242 s. B2's changed air-to-ground command retains a 302 s duration. B1 retains its air-to-air replacement sequence. Patched and unpatched control reruns both give 342 s in the current runtime, compared with the archived 341 s. This 1 s reproduction difference is unrelated to deduplication. We retain the archived cell because the correction does not change the command sequence. These regressions made no LLM calls. The original B4b traces contain genuine command replacement and remain unchanged.

Table S5, main Figure 6 and Supplementary Figure S2 update only the affected B0 cell. D60 tests a specified event order; it does not establish a universal transport-delay threshold. Backend wall-clock time remains separate from controlled simulation waiting.

\subsection{Physical transfer semantics and offline added-time sensitivity}

The model replaces service assignments without tracking individual payloads. New air candidates route through the mission origin. Interrupted-service candidates route through V3 before the destination, but the supervisor can dispatch a backup before the original carrier reaches V3. Preemption interrupts and detaches the incumbent mission. Ground takeover starts a service timer based on the current reference corridor ETA. The model has no explicit states for unloading, rendezvous, loading, new inventory or re-shipment.

We evaluate added transfer time through an \emph{offline trajectory-fixed sensitivity}, rather than a new closed-loop experiment. For an actually affected E2 mission with a post-failure DISPATCH, REASSIGN or GROUND\_FALLBACK, we add one hypothesized transfer delay $h$ to recorded completion. The rule is the same for every policy; uninterrupted services receive no added time. A previously timely completion becomes late when $0\le d_m-c_m<h$. Choices, fault exposure and later scheduling remain fixed. The values $h=15,30,60$ s are illustrative assumptions, not measured handling durations.

At Site B, 80 affected replacements per coordinated policy are timely with 19 s remaining; adding 30 s makes all 80 late. More than 88 s also causes the emergency to miss its deadline. Adding the same handling time to both initial alternatives preserves their speed difference, so this threshold concerns an additional preemption step specifically. These checks identify conclusions that depend on omitted stages. They do not validate dynamic rescheduling with payload transfer.

\subsection{Road-statistics boundary}

The original morphology routine summed all imported passenger edges and divided by 10.24 km$^2$, without clipping them to its bbox argument. The revised calculation intersects each edge polyline with the configured geographic rectangle after projection through the SUMO network transform. It apportions SUMO edge lengths by the fraction of the polyline inside the rectangle and uses the same polygon's area for density. Intersection counts include only nodes inside the polygon, but count their distinct neighbors in the full graph to avoid artificial boundary intersections. Original full-network topology, OD samples and routing remain separately identified and unchanged. Site C still has higher intersection density than A; its description instead draws on dead ends, degree, circuity and facility distance.
